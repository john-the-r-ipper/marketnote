The most important story in e-commerce right now is not a new feature on a big marketplace. It is that AI agents are starting to buy things *on behalf of* consumers — and some of the biggest retailers are fighting back. [The Verge reports](https://www.theverge.com/report/1015811/ai-shopping-agents-blocked-amazon-the-realreal-target-meta-muse) that Amazon has blocked Meta's Muse agent from shopping on its site, that Target's bot checks have stopped agents one day and let them through the next, and that retailers are scrambling to decide whether to welcome, tolerate, or wall off these automated shoppers.

For a small seller, this is not a curiosity. It is a signal about how your store will be discovered, sold, and served in the next few years. Here is what is actually happening — and how to prepare.

## What the Verge reporting shows

Meta, Google, and OpenAI have all launched some version of an agentic shopping tool. The idea is simple: instead of you browsing, an agent does the browsing, comparing, and sometimes the buying for you. But buying requires a seller willing to sell — and that is where it gets messy.

The most prominent friction point is Amazon. The retail giant sued Perplexity over its shopping agent and has blocked Meta's Muse. An Amazon spokesperson told The Verge that third-party applications that offer to buy on behalf of customers "should operate openly and respect service provider decisions about whether or not to participate." Amazon, notably, runs its *own* agentic shopping feature that can auto-reorder and monitor prices. So the fight is not about agents in general — it is about who controls the relationship.

Even where agents are allowed, access is inconsistent. The Verge's editor asked Muse to find a face moisturizer and was caught in Target's bot check one day, only to get through the next. She asked for mattress recommendations and was blocked again minutes after accessing the same site. Agents also run into shipping reality: one search surfaced a grocery chain that would not ship to her address. Meanwhile, The RealReal explicitly says it stays open to third-party agents but updated its bot defenses after an agent was used to bulk-add items to a cart, holding 20-minute reservations that locked out other shoppers.

In short: AI shopping agents are real, but the landscape is uneven, contested, and still forming.

## Why this matters more for small sellers

Big retailers have the leverage to negotiate with agent companies directly. A small seller does not. You are more likely to be on the receiving end of whatever the big players decide — which is why your best move is to make your store *easy and attractive for agents to use correctly*, while protecting yourself from the abusive patterns The Verge documented.

There is also a larger trend underneath this: the customer relationship is splitting into pieces. As [Forbes' Stephen Wunker explains](https://www.forbes.com/sites/stephenwunker/2026/10/25/ai-agents-are-splitting-the-customer-relationship-into-pieces/), agentic commerce divides the relationship into discovery, authority to spend, recourse, and fulfillment. Discovery already happens outside your checkout. The question is whether your products surface in an agent's shortlist at all.

## How to prepare: a small seller's checklist

1. **Make product data machine-readable.** An agent cannot "see" your store the way a human does. It reads structured data — clean titles, descriptions, prices, stock levels, sizes, shipping policies, and images with accurate alt text. If your product feed is messy, an agent will either skip you or misrepresent your goods.
2. **Be findable by agents, not just by search.** Agents pull from sitemaps, product feeds, and structured markup. Keep your catalog server-side and current, and make sure your site is not blocking legitimate bots.
3. **Keep prices and availability accurate.** Agents compare aggressively. A wrong stock level or stale price is not just a lost customer — it is an agent that learns to avoid you.
4. **Protect your catalog from abuse.** The RealReal's cart-hoarding incident shows why you need safeguards against automated bulk actions, while still welcoming legitimate agents. Rate limits and common-sense bot defenses can do both.
5. **Prepare for chat-first ordering.** Agents and buyers increasingly transact in conversation. If you can capture an order in the chat where the customer already is — rather than forcing a move to a cart page — you reduce friction that agents and humans alike feel. This is the core idea behind chat-commerce tools like [OnlyOrders](https://onlyorders.tech), which turn DMs and comments into confirmed orders.
6. **Decide what you are fighting for.** Per the Forbes analysis, merchants hold the strongest asset in fulfillment and service. Even if an agent finds the product and a bank approves the spend, the *record of the sale and the customer service after it* is still yours. That is where you build the trust that keeps the customer coming back.

## FAQ: AI shopping agents and your store

**Will agents hurt my sales?**
Not necessarily. Agents can bring you buyers you would never otherwise reach — but only if your data is clean enough for them to represent you correctly. The risk is being invisible or misrepresented, not being shopped.

**Should I block agents like Amazon does?**
Blocking is a strategic choice made by platforms protecting a relationship. For a small seller, being findable is usually more valuable than being protected. Focus on accurate data and abuse safeguards rather than a blanket ban.

**What is the difference between an agent and a normal shopper?**
An agent is a program that browses, compares, and sometimes buys on behalf of a human. It is faster, more repetitive, and more dependent on structured data than a human shopper — which is why your product feed quality suddenly matters so much.

**Do I need to build an AI agent?**
No. You need to be *ready for* agents on the other side of the transaction. That takes data hygiene, not software development. Building your own conversational layer — like chat-first ordering — is a separate, optional step that can help you capture the relationship early.

## Bottom line

In 2026, AI shopping agents are moving from demo to reality — and the big retailers are already deciding who gets in. For a small seller, the practical takeaway is not to panic or to build an agent. It is to get your product data clean, keep your catalog accurate, protect against abuse, and be ready to transact where the conversation happens. The businesses that are findable, accurate, and chat-ready will be the ones agents recommend first.
